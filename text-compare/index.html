Text Compare: a directional review of two versions

Text Compare highlights differences between an earlier and a revised text so a reviewer can focus on what changed. It does not decide which wording is correct or merge the versions automatically. This Text Compare guide explains input direction, moved paragraphs and formatting noise, then follows a short instruction revision where a small change can matter more than the total number of highlighted lines.

01Text Compare: a directional review of two versions

This page compares two pieces of text so you can inspect additions, removals and changed lines. It is useful for reviewing a revised document, configuration snippet or message. Put the earlier version on the left and the proposed version on the right. A difference view is directional. Reversing the inputs reverses what counts as added or removed. Label the sources before interpreting a summary, especially when the result will inform a review discussion or an approval decision.

Begin Text Compare by identifying which document is the earlier version and which is the proposed revision. Place the earlier text on the left and the revision on the right, then keep that direction consistent while interpreting additions and removals. Reversing the inputs changes the meaning of those labels. Text Compare is especially useful when a reviewer has a focused question, such as whether a requirement changed or a missing instruction was added. It does not supply the authority to approve either version. Keep the source documents and their dates available so the visible comparison can be traced to the actual files or messages under review.

02Text Compare: line differences are not meaning differences

Line-based comparison works with the supplied text rather than understanding its intent. A moved paragraph may appear as a removal and an addition. That is not necessarily evidence that its meaning was deleted; inspect the surrounding context. Whitespace and line endings can influence how changes are presented. Keep source formatting consistent when the question concerns wording, but preserve it when spaces or indentation carry meaning. Decide which kind of review you are performing before modifying either input.

Interpret Text Compare as a line-oriented view of supplied text. A single word can change an instruction's meaning, while a large highlighted block can result from moving the same paragraph. The number of changed lines is therefore not a measure of importance. Text Compare also cannot infer whether two differently phrased sentences are equivalent or whether a rearrangement preserves a legal, technical or editorial requirement. Read the full relevant passage on both sides, including surrounding conditions. If spaces or indentation matter to the material, preserve them during review. If wrapping differences are merely presentation noise, diagnose that separately rather than assuming every highlighted line represents a substantive edit.

03Text Compare: an instruction with a changed condition

Try a short instruction where one sentence changes and another is added. Check the highlighted passages and read the complete new instruction. A small difference can change the meaning more than a large block of purely editorial rearrangement. Comparison runs locally in the browser. The tool does not merge versions automatically or decide which wording is correct. Use the result as evidence for a human review, then edit the authoritative document in its normal workflow.

For a Text Compare example, take an instruction that originally says to proceed after a check and revise it to require a different condition before proceeding. Add one explanatory sentence elsewhere. Inspect the changed condition carefully even if it occupies fewer highlighted characters than the added explanation. Then read the complete revised instruction to see whether the sequence still makes sense. This Text Compare exercise emphasizes consequence over volume. A review should describe what behavior the revision changes, not simply report that two lines were added. Keep the original until the responsible person has accepted the wording through the normal document workflow rather than treating the comparison screen as an automatic approval.

04Text Compare: when formatting overwhelms the review

If the result shows nearly every line changed, check input order, line wrapping and whether one source includes extra headers. A formatting transformation can create noise that hides the substantive change. A smaller excerpt can help diagnose the difference. Keep both originals until the review is complete. A summary count cannot replace reading important changed passages, particularly for instructions or configuration. Use a format-specific tool when JSON structure or spreadsheet cells require additional interpretation.

If Text Compare marks nearly everything as changed, inspect how the two versions were copied or exported. One may contain line wrapping, extra headings or line endings that differ from the other. A formatting transformation can obscure a small wording change. Use a short excerpt to identify the source of noise while keeping authoritative originals untouched. Text Compare should not be made quieter by deleting meaningful whitespace or content merely to reduce highlights. Decide whether the task is reviewing words, source formatting or both. That choice determines which normalization, if any, is justified before comparing and what information must remain visible for the reviewer.

05Text Compare: moved passages and unchanged context

A paragraph moved in Text Compare may appear once as a deletion and once as an addition. Search for the passage in the other version before concluding that its content was removed. However, relocation can still matter if the surrounding conditions or heading change its interpretation. Text Compare does not decide whether moving a warning below a procedure makes the document acceptable, for example. Read the new context and explain the effect in the review. This is also why a clean or short difference summary cannot replace checking critical unchanged passages: a requirement can become misleading through its new position even when its exact words remain the same.

06Text Compare: turning highlights into a review decision

Use Text Compare output to guide a human decision and then make edits in the authoritative document. The tool does not merge competing versions or establish which source is current. For configuration, validate the resulting settings with the application; for structured data, consider a format-specific comparison to reduce irrelevant representation differences. Text Compare performs the comparison locally, but pasted material remains sensitive if you later share the highlights or source. Select a focused excerpt when discussing private content. Retain both versions until the review is complete, and document the reason for accepting a meaningful change so a later reader understands more than the count of added or removed lines.

Text Compare questions

Which side should contain the original in Text Compare?

Put the earlier version on the left and the revision on the right in Text Compare. Additions and removals are directional, so reversing the inputs reverses their interpretation. Label or record the sources when the result supports an approval or review discussion.

Does Text Compare understand whether meaning changed?

Text Compare highlights textual differences and does not determine semantic equivalence or correctness. A small word change can matter greatly, while a moved paragraph can create a large difference. Read the relevant context and apply the document's actual requirements.

Why does Text Compare highlight almost every line?

Text Compare can show extensive differences when line wrapping, headings or formatting vary between inputs. Inspect a short sample to identify the cause. Preserve meaningful whitespace and authoritative originals rather than deleting content simply to obtain a smaller-looking difference.

Can Text Compare merge two documents automatically?

Text Compare supports review rather than automatic merging or approval. Use its highlights to understand the changes, then edit the authoritative document through your normal workflow. Keep both sources until the chosen result has been checked for the task it must perform.
